# 16 / Spore

2026-11-16

Crawl over an endless moss carpet with a worm's eye, at dusk in autumn, and throw spores. Day 05's moss, taken out of the white studio and laid over a forest floor that never ends: dark glittering wood-moss and haircap over bare humus, the sun nine degrees over the horizon ahead of you so every frond tip glows. Small fungi push up through the moss, wet and glistening: yellowfoot, crested coral, chanterelles, puffballs and fly agarics, all of them at home in mossy Swedish spruce forest. Ferns arch over the moss in stands you crawl in under, turned gold and rust, glowing when the low sun comes through them; you start under one. Spruce trunks rise out of the moss into a golden mist that lies on the ground. The plants that stood here before (grass, fallen leaves and needles, spore stalks, branches, boulders) were taken out to be done again from scratch, ferns and trunks first.

Drag to turn your head: you drag the world, across and up and down alike (a mouse pointing up and down, up looking up, went against its own sideways drag). Hold still to crawl where you look, drag while holding to steer (left turns left, up looks up); on a phone, held low on the screen (the bottom fifth) you back up instead. A tap or a click sows a pinch of spores where you clicked, where that point's ray meets the moss (higher on the screen further off, to the side to the side) but 45 cm to 1.6 m away (closer, they grew up out of focus); with the pointer locked, at the sight: no throw, a soft cloud let go just over the moss that settles there, there at once (puffs near full size from the start; the burst's swell from small read as a second's wait). To fling one further, hold the mouse button and let go: the slingshot in your hand is drawn back and lets fly (the ring of the sight opens as it winds up), flick a finger up (quick and mostly straight; the look it dragged goes back; a finger's long press is the crawl): it flies at the sight, locked in the middle of the screen, and arcs over the moss leaving a faint wisp of smoke, bursts where it lands, and leaves a pale band of smoke lying over the moss that slowly fades; when the cloud settles, a cluster of whatever you picked in the fungarium pushes up there, slowly, one after another, the tips feeling their way round as they grow, and it stays (only past a few hundred does the oldest wither back into the moss). Tap the packet (or F) for the fungarium, a little field notebook: it opens on a page with a grid, one cell per species, and a tap on one is what your spores grow from then on; the small magnifier in a cell's corner turns the page to watch it grow up close, turning slowly, grow it again, and throw it. You hold a kraft-paper packet of spores at the bottom right of the screen, in your right hand, all of it in view (a little larger on a desktop; it was in the middle, bigger and cut off by the bottom edge), its label the specimen it grows, like a shooter's weapon: 1 to 5 (up to 9 and 0 as species come), the wheel swaps it, dropping it out of view and bringing the next one up as in Quake; shift and a number plants that species' specimen at the sight. You are a little forest gnome on two feet: a step each side, the eye lifting and dipping over each, swaying to the foot the weight is on, the head looking slowly about as you go; standing, a slow breath. Space hops (about nine centimetres, the knees giving a little as you land), C crouches, the eye down among the frond tips, creeping at half the pace, and shift runs at twice it. Just looking, a drag turns the world round; while crawling a drag steers, so drag left to turn left, and held out to the side you keep turning that way, like a stick, so a hairpin needs no second swipe. Crawl straight into a trunk and you climb it, up to two metres: on the bark you crawl where you look (facing the trunk, forward is up; facing away, down; to the side, round it), and you can look straight down at the moss and the ferns from above; climb down to the moss and you are off. On a desktop the pill bottom centre (or L) locks the pointer: the mouse itself is the look as in a shooter, a click sows, holding and letting go flings, esc lets go; with a mouse you crawl with the keys. On a desktop the keys are listed bottom left, in thin outlined caps, dim until hovered (they were a line in the title plate; a phone, which has no key row, still has its line there). With the pointer locked, A and D (and the side arrows) step sideways instead of turning, as in a shooter. W A S D or the arrows work too (shift runs, twice the pace; it crouched for a while, now C does), and on a phone the button top right turns your head with the phone. `?walk` crawls by itself.

## How

- **Chunks** (`world.ts`): the floor is built in 1 × 1 squares around you, three levels of detail by distance: fine grids with 13k fronds near you; coarser grids with a few fronds further out; coarse shells only at the edge of the haze. Chunks behind you go, the ones ahead are built one per frame, nearest and in front first. Every field (height, moss, species, pillows, clumps) is a function of the world position, so chunks meet without seams and one rebuilt later comes back the same. Normals come from the height field by central differences, the moss's smoother normal from a wider stencil, so nothing needs the neighbours.
- **Moss**: day 05's engine: a shell undercoat (10 noise shells here, 16 in day 05; per-species height, density, sheen and palette blended by five weights per vertex) and instanced fronds bent along beziers in the vertex shader, at day 05's 175 % size. Nothing is painted: coverage, age and species come from noise fields, and each chunk fills its frond age texture once.
- **Dew** (`dew.ts`): drops caught in the moss at the frond tips, sitting in the folds of fern pinnae, hanging under their tips. Each drop is a quad turned to the eye and shaded as a small sphere of water: a dark rim where light bends away, a dim refracted inside, a hard glint of the sun off the front, and looking towards the sun a gold spot of light gathered through it, stronger in some drops than others. Never smaller than about a pixel and a half; the ones that would be fade instead of flickering. Premultiplied blending, so the body darkens what is behind and the glints add on top; the lens turns the ones out of focus into sparkling discs. Wet everything else: a sharp sheen on the fronds, the fern pinnae and the mushroom caps.
- **One for phones**: a spore particle dying during a frame has a life just below zero, and `Math.pow(negative, 1.5)` is NaN. That NaN alpha went to the GPU for one frame per particle: desktop GPUs dropped those pixels, an iPhone drew each one as an opaque black disc, so every throw left a blinking stream of black balls. The life is clamped at zero now. (Reversed `smoothstep` edges, undefined in GLSL, were fixed along the way too.)
- **Mist**: on top of the haze, a ground mist: an exponential layer 0.35 units deep integrated along each view ray from the camera's height to the hit's, lit with the haze's colour, so it glows towards the sun and thins out above the moss. Right around you the air is clear: the mist starts 0.6 units out and eases in over the next metre or so.
- **Light and haze, after the splash**: the splash's look (a light, bright haze that keeps its contrast deep in, the trunks standing in layers, the sun coming through between them, the greens and oranges their own colours) asked for less air, not more. The fog went from 0.22 to 0.06 and the ground mist from 0.9 to 0.3: thick and golden, everything past two metres had melted into one yellow wall. The sky and the haze toward the sun are a paler cream (less saturated gold all round, the glow round the sun tighter), and the grade leans unsaturated midtones only half as far to amber. Looking away from the sun the wood now stands many trunks deep.
- **Beams and motes, after the splash**: the screen-space shafts now count the open sky to a power (2.5), so the trunks and fronds cut clean wedges out of them, beams rather than a glow, brighter (1.1) and reaching further from the sun on screen; softer right at the sun, where looking straight into it the full beams washed everything to white. And glowing motes, the splash's gold orbs: a second set of the dust (`dust.ts`, `motes`), 140 (60 on a phone) in a box nine metres round, a few millimetres each, a bright core in a soft halo, rising slowly and twinkling, glimmering a little in the shade too; none closer than half a metre (there it was one big blurred disc).
- **Beams in the air** (`post.ts`, with the morning sun): the screen-space shafts need the sun on the screen, and at 45 degrees it mostly is not. So the haze pass also steps along each view ray, 28 times (12 on a phone) out to thirty metres, closer together near the eye, asking at each step whether the sun gets down through the crowns there: the same field of pools and spots the floor is lit by (`canopyAt`), projected down along the light, but only its pools and larger spots, softly, and the same steps for every pixel (with the finest spots and a start jittered per pixel the beams came out as a dither, white noise as grain and an interleaved gradient as a fine hatching; smooth, they do not band), so the columns of light in the air land where the light lies on the moss. Lit air is made brighter and shaded air darker, the difference from the average, strongest toward the sun (fine haze scatters forward): beams through the wood from anywhere, light and dark. The air is far thicker low down, a mist on the moss (and the mist itself thicker, 0.3 to 0.55), so the beams light up most where they reach it.
- **Dusk**: the sun at nine degrees, ahead and to the right. The light carries a low-sun colour through the grade, the shade a cooling violet-blue sky fill, the back light is twice as strong and golden. Light shafts in the atmosphere pass: each pixel marches 24 steps towards the sun on screen through the depth, counting how much of the way is open sky or far haze, so mushrooms and the moss's own rise cut dark wedges out of the glow; they fade out as the sun goes behind you.
- **Morning** (after the splash): the sun now stands 45 degrees up in the same quarter, its rays coming down from above through the crowns; whiter than at dusk, the air paler and a little cooler (`?sun=9` brings back the dusk the day began with, a low sun straight through the trunks; anything between too).
- **Near the eye**: fronds dissolve in a fine dither when they come within a few centimetres, instead of filling the frame as big flat blurred shapes.
- **Autumn**: the moss palettes went down to a deep dark green, the air to a golden brown, the sun warmer, the sky fill less blue, and the grade warms the highlights.
- **Light**: a 4096 shadow map over the 6 units ahead of you, re-rendered every 0.3 units you crawl, snapped to whole texels so the shadows do not crawl with you, and without the fronds and stalks, too small to matter in it. A canopy field projected along the sun and swaying slowly breaks the light into pools and spots; shade drops to 22 %. The back light is the sun itself and only shows looking into it.
- **Lens**: GTAO at half resolution, then two full-screen passes on the beauty target's depth. The first rebuilds the world ray per pixel for a height and sun-aware haze and a plain golden sky behind it all, glowing broadly on the sun's side and brighter towards it (it had bright canopy gaps, yellow and orange, but between the trunks they read as patches cut out of paper), and writes the view depth into alpha; the air fades everything into the very colour of the sky behind (it faded to a greyer haze, so far trunks stood out as grey bars against the gold), thin enough that trunk after trunk shows going off into it, and whatever the sun lights clearly brighter than the air sees it thinner by up to two fifths (a cheat: a trunk's sunny side a few steps off was lost in the haze like its shaded one; keeping a share of it at any distance left far trunks standing out of the fog). The second gathers a 40-tap golden-angle depth of field (thin lens, the background never bleeding over sharp foreground), then grades: cool shadows, warm highlights, a vignette. The focus is where the view ray first hits the moss or a mushroom, eased like a lens hunting. The autofocus marches a ray out from the eye and stops at the moss, a trunk or a fungus; it only tries the fungi within 3 m and the trunks within 8 (trying every cluster at every step was the cost with many sown).
- **Ants** (`ants.ts`): carpenter ants (hästmyra, the biggest in Sweden, about 14 mm, black with a dark red thorax, nesting in spruce trunks) on trails from half the trunks near you, by hash: out over the moss's top 1.4 to 3 m, wandering either side of a straight line, and up the bark 1.8 m on the side facing it, drifting round a little. A trail is sampled every centimetre with its surface normal (the ground's slope, or out of the bark, using the trunk's own radius function). Fourteen to twenty-four ants a metre, each with its own lane a few millimetres off the line and its own pace, turning back at the ends, now and then stopping a moment. One instanced mesh: ellipsoids for gaster, petiole, thorax, head and mandibles, three-sided tubes for six legs and two elbowed antennae; the legs step in two tripods half a stride apart, swinging about their roots and lifting, the antennae feel about, all in the vertex shader from a per-instance stride phase. Glossy chitin, the moss's light and shadow. Not drawn past 4.5 m, nor into the shadow map. They are small at a worm's height: on the bark they show as a file of dark bodies; on the moss they are half hidden among the leaves, as they would be.
- **Sun at your back** (a cheat): with a sun nine degrees up, its long shadows cover most of the floor and front light barely reaches the moss's very dark top, so looking away from the sun was a black carpet while looking into it everything glowed. Now whatever is turned to the sun catches a warm gold of its own (not its dark colour lit), scaled by how much the sun is at your back: the strands' tips, the velvet under them, the fronds' leaves, fern pinnae and the fungi. In the shadows a few flecks still get through the fern lace (a finer noise projected along the light). The canopy lets a little more sun through, the deepest shade is 20% instead of 14%, and the fungi's fill and wet sheen follow the shade like the rest.
- **The wood's sounds** (`forest.ts`): made on the spot with Web Audio too, no files. A bed of wind in the spruces, low, under the rest (louder it was a gale), in stereo (pinkish noise, twelve seconds of it, the two channels apart so it is wide): a low roar of the whole wood through a low-pass and the hiss of the needles in a band round 4 kHz over it, both breathing in gusts every few seconds (the roar swelling and opening, the hiss rising) and falling away. And birds of a northern spruce wood on an autumn evening, one every two to six seconds by weight, each calling from a place of its own (kept a while, so a robin sings again from its tree), placed in 3D with HRTF so they stay put as you turn your head, the listener being the eye, and the far ones duller as well as quieter (a low-pass closing with distance, as the air takes the highs). Robin (rödhake), the autumn singer: a thin, wistful warble of slides, trills and ticks, wandering down. Wren (gärdsmyg): loud and fast, a run, a trill, a run. Goldcrest (kungsfågel): three to five very high thin zii's, close, up in a spruce. Great tit (talgoxe): ti-ta ti-ta. Blackbird (koltrast): the dusk alarm, a run of chinks quickening. Tawny owl (kattuggla), far off: the male's long hoot, a pause, a short hu, a quickening hu-hu-hu and the long wavering huuuu; each hoot round and breathy (a narrow band of noise on the note), sliding up into its pitch, holding and sagging, the last one's waver a slow, deep wobble of pitch and loudness coming on as it goes (a fast flutter on a bare tone sounded like a machine); or the female's ke-wick, nasal, softened. Great spotted woodpecker (större hackspett): a sharp kik, or a drum roll on a dead trunk. And, out of season (they are gone south by autumn, but too much a Swedish wood to leave out), rare and far: the cuckoo (gök), ko-ko, as measured off a recording: the first note short and arched (575 up to 600 and down to 530 Hz in 0.16 s), the second a third of a second later, a flat 490 for a quarter second, both nearly pure, one ko-ko every 1.6 to 1.8 seconds (it had been a little high, twice as quick and coloured with overtones); the nightingale (näktergal) in phrases, slow swelling whistles, a fast rattle of chucks, the deep jug-jug, a buzzing trill, a run of sweet slides. The birds are kept well down and well off, ten metres and more, the far ones out to ninety (nearer and louder they sounded like birds at your ear, not the wood round you). And the ground close round you, more often than the birds (every quarter second to two): clusters of dry ticks in the needles and leaves, uneven in spacing and strength, a rustle of soft swells of hiss with crackles in them, now and then a twig snapping (a sharp crack, a short woody ring, the broken end giving a moment later), each from somewhere new one to nine metres off on the floor. Your own body, unplaced, close: a footfall each time the eye comes down in the gait, mostly a soft hiss of fronds brushed with a faint pad and a few stalk crackles under it, a hair left and right in turn, softer crouching (a thud was too heavy for a gnome on moss); a landing two of them, harder; and the breath, in as the eye rises and out as it falls, air through the nose, rising going in, lower and longer going out, quiet, deeper walking and for a while after a hop. Each a few oscillators with frequency and gain envelopes scheduled from a start time, so they render offline as well. Now and then (every quarter to almost a minute) a branch cracks somewhere fifteen to forty metres off, loud and ringing through the wood (it sends the most into the echo): first the fibres tearing in a run of small cracks closing up, then the break, one dry crack, all noise and no note in it (a ring under it sounded like a spring), the splinters after, and sometimes the limb rattling down through the twigs below and onto the moss. Everything goes into the wood's echo, a convolution reverb whose impulse response is made from noise on the spot: a handful of early reflections off the nearest trunks in the first 80 ms, then a sparse, soft tail (only a third of its samples at full strength: trunks, not a hall), the highs dying first, 1.6 s to fall 60 dB, the two channels apart so it comes back wide. The far birds send the most (a far bird is mostly echo), the ground's crackles and the throws, puffs and creaks a little, your own steps and breath hardly any.
- **Soundboard** (`soundboard.ts`, `?debug`): to try the sounds one by one: every sound on a button (each bird, the ground's, the crack, your steps, landing and breath, the throw's whoosh and puffs, each fungus's creak), where it comes from (any side, or left, right, ahead or behind, so far off), a slider for each part's level (master, wind, birds, ground, cracks, body, echo) and switches for what plays by itself, to hear one thing alone. Its clicks and keys stay in it.
- **Dust** (`dust.ts`): specks hanging in the air over the moss, drifting slowly with a curl of their own, a little over a thousand (half on a phone) in a box round the eye three metres across and one high, wrapped into it in the shader so the box goes where you go and nothing is updated on the CPU. Each takes the light the floor would at its place (the shadow map and the canopy's spots), brightest in the beams and only a faint glimmer in the shade (lit only in the sun, near the ground the low sun leaves most of the air in shadow and the dust was all but gone), glows looking into the sun (fine dust scatters forward) and twinkles as it tumbles. The ones right in front of the eye are drawn as soft discs of light with a slightly brighter rim, out of focus as motes in a beam are in a photo; further off a speck of a couple of pixels. Drawn after the atmosphere pass like the spores, testing itself against the scene's depth, added light.
- **Sound** (`sound.ts`): made on the spot with Web Audio, no files. A fling whooshes: looped white noise through a band-pass sweeping from 500 Hz up to 2.4 kHz and down again over the flight, swelling and fading. Where it lands and where you sow, a puff: noise through a low-pass closing from a couple of kHz to 220 Hz in a quarter to half a second, with a little sine thump falling from about 130 to 48 Hz under it; a sowing is softer than a landing. Quieter with distance and panned to where it happens. Fungi growing near you creak now and then (unevenly: each waits a random while, now and then two or three in a run; while they are between three and ninety-seven per cent grown), the way a stalk does when it bends: stick-slip, a run of sharp little ticks written sample by sample, uneven in spacing and strength, the gaps closing as the strain builds, very uneven (each gap drawn from a wide log-normal spread, a pause now and then where the strain holds, a double tick now and then, strength mostly faint with a few hard catches), rung through two narrow bands, slow and low (a gliding sawtooth through one band buzzed rather than creaked, and the first ticks were too quick and high), in a voice per kind: a thin squeak for yellowfoot, tiny ticks for crested coral, a soft creak for chanterelles, a deep one for fly agarics, a woody one for karljohan, a high one for the waxcap; noise instead for a puffball's pop, a slippery jack's squelch and a morel's dry crackle (the noise chopped into specks). The context is made on the first tap or key, as browsers want. A round button top right (left of the gyro on a phone), or M, mutes all of it: a gain after the master, so the soundboard's master stays as set, faded over a few hundredths of a second, and remembered in the browser for the next visit; the soundboard sits under it.
- **Grade**: one look over everything, at the end of the lens pass: greens pulled towards olive and calmed (the moss was a loud lime next to the golden ferns), unsaturated midtones, the haze, leaned amber instead of a flat green-grey, shadows toned a deep blue-teal and highlights a cream gold, a soft S on the luminance with a rounded shoulder so sunlit fronds do not burn out. The packet in your hand is graded with it in CSS: dimmer, warmer, a little sepia, its label off-white, a warm glow from the sun at its top left and shade at the bottom.
- **Climbing**: crawling into a trunk head on (pushed off it, facing it within about 40°, not looking down) puts you on it: an angle round it and a height over its foot. The eye sits a worm's height off the bark, from the same radius function the trunk's geometry is built with (root flare and buttresses included), and eases there, so getting on is a move. Forward is split by where you look: into the bark is up, away is down, sideways is round, plus the look's pitch; 0.7 times the pace on the ground. You can look down to about 83° while on it. Climbing down past the moss puts you back on the ground; the not-looking-down rule keeps it from catching you straight away again.
- **Crawling**: a worm's eye just over the frond tips, following the ground, with a slow heave and roll while moving. Mushroom stems are solid: you slide round them. A press becomes a crawl after 220 ms if it has not moved; past 8 px it is a head turn; a short press that did neither is a tap. With a mouse, dragging up looks up; with a finger, it drags the world (up looks down). A screen height of drag turns about twice the field of view (it was 1.2, too slow for a U-turn); crawling, the finger's distance sideways from where it went down, past 14 px, also turns you at up to 2.4 rad/s, full at 30% of the short side. The keys turn at 2.2 rad/s. With the pointer locked, mouse movement turns the head directly (1.6 fields of view per screen height), and the press is only ever a tap or a crawl.
- **Ferns** (`ferns.ts`): a crown of 6 to 11 fronds, each a quadratic bezier up out of the crown, arching over and sinking at the tip, the outer ones lower. A thin tapering rachis, then 16 pinnae down each side, alternating, longest a third of the way up; each pinna a strip three columns wide, folded up along its midrib and curving down under its own weight, about five times as long as wide. The pinnule outline is cut in the fragment shader from (along, across): lobes leaning towards the tip, cut almost to the midrib, a few teeth on each, so the edge is crisp without a vertex per lobe. Autumn per frond, the tips ahead of the base: green, gold, rust, brown. Under each pinnule, rows of rust spore dots (sori). Light from behind comes through. The air moves the fronds, more at the tip. Placed by hash in 2.6 unit cells, two to five to a stand, fronds up to two units long arching high enough to crawl under, merged into one mesh per cell, in the shadow map; only the tight bunch of stipes at a crown is solid. Two levels of detail: cells further than 4.5 units get half the pinnae, each nearly twice as wide, and fewer rows, rebuilt one cell per frame as you come and go.
- **Ferns coming and going** (after the splash lightened the haze): stands placed seven metres off and swapped to full detail at four and a half had been hidden in the thick golden air; in the light haze they popped up as you walked. Now placed out to 12 m (9 on a phone) with a third, cheap level past 8 m: the whole frond one card along its rachis, its opposite pinnae cut in the outline by the shader (just the outline where they would be finer than a pixel), and none of it pops: a new stand dissolves in over 0.8 s through a fixed dither on the screen, and a new level of detail dissolves in over the old one while the old dissolves out under it, the same dither mirrored, so between them every pixel is drawn once. The shared material's fade is set just before each stand is drawn.
- **Ferns, finer** (after a lady fern in autumn, then a closer photo): twenty eight pinnules a pinna, nearly touching, oblong with round blunt tips, cut not quite to the midrib and faintly toothed (eight broad lobes had read as oak leaves, fourteen pointed ones as spiky), the pinnae wide enough to touch their neighbours, smoothing to the pinna's outline where a pinnule is only a few pixels; straw, copper and brown instead of a bright gold (a saturated yellow lit up every edge), the midrib and veins pale on a green frond and dark on a turned one. Each frond is still green or has turned orange and copper, hardly ever between, both side by side in a stand, more turned the further on it is (all of them in between had made the stands one straw gold). And curvier: the rachis a cubic bezier up out of the crown, arching over, its tip hooking down but staying over the moss (a quadratic bends once and read stiff; sunk below the crown, whole fronds had lain on the ground), the stipe bare for its first quarter, the stands a quarter smaller, the pinnae in opposite pairs, dense (36 a side near, 18 in the middle distance, nearly overlapping), each running straight out from the rachis for its first third, then drooping under its weight and sweeping toward the frond's tip, a sickle; a pinnule right at the rachis and the base whole (curving from the rachis on, and starting on a notch with only a hairline midrib, the pinnae had looked as if they floated beside the stalk).
- **Trunks** (`trees.ts`): one spruce in about seven of ten cells of a 3.6 unit grid (it was under half; more give layer behind layer going off into the fog, for depth), by hash, out to 34 units. A unit trunk with its root flare (buttresses from two sines, sinking into the ground, rings packed towards the foot) in three levels of detail, 48 × 40, 16 × 14 and 8 × 3, each an instanced mesh with the same material; trunks are sorted into them every half step you take. The bark is all shader, spruce, after a photo of one: thin round scales two or three centimetres across overlapping like roof tiles, a cell noise with a whole number of cells around so it wraps without a seam, its cells warped by a noise sampled on a circle (so it wraps too) so the scales come out curved and uneven. Each scale rises to its lower edge, which stands off the trunk: where it lies over the scale below a dark gap shows, beside its neighbours only a faint parting, so they read as tiles and not a net of outlines (outlined all round they looked like crazy paving). One in thirty has come away, red-brown underneath. Dark grey, a shade different each, weathered unevenly up close with pale flaky patches and dark pits. The normal comes from finite differences of that height along the bark, and fades out with the relief and contrast as the scales shrink under a pixel, where the extra lookups are skipped. (It was pine, long plates and deep furrows, before that a uniform cell pattern that read as a tortoise shell; the plates looked fake up close.) Green algae on the shaded side, pale crust lichen. Up close, once a few millimetres are bigger than a pixel, the scales get a grain of their own: short fibres, small papery flakes and crumbs, bump-mapped and darkening between the fibres. Each trunk stands on a little hill of the ground's own moss: the ground height has a rounded rise at every trunk, a bit over ten centimetres at the foot of a big one and gone 90 cm past the bark, so the moss carpet heaps up and climbs the foot a little. The trunks are looked up by hash in the ground's own module so it knows where they stand, cached per cell. (A cushion of moss painted onto the bark, swelling its outline, looked like a green rug; the real moss is better.) In the low sun the trunks' sunny side catches a warm gold of its own, smooth, varying a little up the trunk, through the bark's pattern (the bark is nearly black, so the sun alone barely showed; laid on with the canopy's spots it came out as white flakes, a birch). Matte, not plastic: the lichen comes in specks, the sun does not reach into the gaps, and the rim of light from the sun behind is weak and broken up. Solid, root flare included; the autofocus takes the flare at its average, since with the full buttress it focused in the air in front of a trunk you stood next to.
- **Birches** (`trees.ts`, after the splash): about one trunk in three a birch, white among the dark spruces, slimmer (0.42 of a spruce's radius) and with less of a root flare (a swelling, not buttresses; the climbing and the ants follow it). Drawn by an instanced mesh of their own, three levels as the spruces, in a branch of the same bark shader. The bark is drawn from what is there, not soft noise (in noise it read sloppy): chalky white going cream, faintly streaked round by the paper's fibres, mottled grey; lenticels as short dark lens-shaped dashes in rows round the trunk, of all lengths, each a little raised with a shaded rim; the paper peeling in thin strips part way round, a shade pinker, the lower edge curled out (a bright lip, a dark line under it); black marks wider than tall, rough edged, crusty and cracked across, in broken bands and chevrons; and at the foot an old birch's black bark in plates split by deep branching cracks, low and uneven, here and there a spike, black flecks thinning out above. The relief is a height, its normal by finite differences, eased out under a pixel. White bark is lit by all round it, a strong fill so it stays white in the shade, but not the black (lit as much it went a lilac grey). The spruces stand where they stood (the birch is drawn after their numbers).
- **Crowns** (`foliage.ts`, after the splash, where the light seeps down through the leaves): spruce boughs and birch twigs on the trunks, from a couple of metres up into the haze; from down in the moss they show mostly ten to thirty metres off, so every trunk in range gets them. Cards with their shape cut in the fragment shader, two instanced meshes. Spruce boughs in whorls a third to half a metre apart from three to five metres up nearly to the top of the trunk, four to six each, a cone: long low down, shorter and shorter to a narrow top, hanging well down and turning up at the tip (whorls to 14 m only had left the trunk a bare pole above, three boughs a whorl had been sparse, and boughs from a metre and a half up filled the worm's eye with dark slabs: an old spruce in a wood has shed its low ones). Near you (within five metres, so the lowest boughs over you, a few hundred at most, dissolving over a metre into the card through a mirrored dither) a bough is a model built once: a branch with side shoots out and forward, the long ones with shoots of their own, needles all round every twig (a bottle brush), about half the shoots gone gold at their outer ends, as in autumn; the gold only there, a detail. Further off a card: a dark mass hanging in clumps, a few holes for the light, its edge a dither the lens blurs into needles. A fishbone of shoots with a clean midrib, cut crisply and gold at the tips, had read from a distance as palm fronds; the low boughs dead and grey. Birch: hanging curtains narrowing to their tips, three twigs with pointed oval leaves along them, jittered and askew, some missing (a grid of even dots had read as a printed pattern), yellow-green going gold, a few brown; thin, so they glow against the sun. Lit by the canopy's own light spots (no shadow map reaches that high) and the sun through them from behind; the light shafts are counted from the depth, so the sun now comes down in beams between them. Not in the shadow map: the floor's light already comes in canopy spots.
- **The lie of the land** (`world.ts` `baseHeight`, after the splash), very discreet: the wood stands on a gentle hillside that falls away to your right as you go toward the sun (across the light, north to south), six percent (three was too discreet to see from the moss), slow swells of half a metre on it, and now and then a low rise, half a metre over ten metres. The moss grows by the local relief, its height over this lie of the land (`land`), not over y = 0: by that, the hollows bare and the hummocks covered, a hillside went bare all the way down. Everything stands on the ground's height, so it all follows; the chunks' bounding spheres are centred at the ground's height now (at y = 0 they would have been culled on a rise), and the ground mist lies on the ground under the eye instead of on y = 0 (uphill it would have thinned away, downhill drowned the hollows).
- **Clearings and giants** (`world.ts` `clearing`, `props.ts`, after the splash): from down in the moss nothing past the first fern stand showed, and fungi further off were small and pale in the haze. So the wood opens now and then: a slow noise, a clearing a few metres across every so often, where the ferns stay out (and stand smaller towards its edge) and most trunks are gone, a birch now and then left at its edge. And in the clearings, on a 2.5 m grid out to 11 m (8 on a phone), groups of giant fly agarics, now and then porcini: one to three clusters a step apart, caps a hand to half a metre across, a fairy tale's and not a field guide's, so from the moss you see out to them standing in the light, as in the splash. Only the kinds that are models, which cost only their drawing: those are seen out to 17 m (11 on a phone), the procedural ones to 9. At the start about 48 of them are drawn, some 400,000 vertices.
- **The floor** (`floor.ts`, after the splash): from a worm's eye the floor is seen edge on, so what gives it life is what stands up; fallen leaves lying flat showed only as slivers, right under you. By hash on a 1 m grid out to 6 m (4.5 on a phone), one cell a frame, each kind one instanced mesh repacked as cells come and go: wood sorrel in dense clumps (18 to 44 plants, three heart-shaped leaflets two or three centimetres long, folded a little, on thin stems seven to fourteen centimetres up out of the moss, a fresh light green that glows against the sun); pixie-cup lichens in groups, three to six centimetres (over the moss's fronds: a fairy tale's, a little), grey-green and granular, a third of the groups red at the rim; reindeer lichen, pale finely branched cushions ten to twenty centimetres across, more in the clearings (toned down: they shone out like stones); stones, lumpy and half sunk, grey with pale crust lichen, moss over their tops and creeping down the sides with a ragged edge, a velvet of tiny shoots lit gold at the tips (bare, they looked dropped there at random; stones and cushions both dark and stained at the foot, unevenly, as things lying in wet ground are, only their tops dried pale; three levels of detail, 3,400 triangles near, 1,000 to four metres, 320 beyond, sorted again as you move: at 320 near they were angular; merged vertices: the icosahedron comes unindexed and its normals had made a faceted shard); and fallen birch leaves, curled, yellow to orange to brown, many under a birch, four in ten wedged on edge in the moss where they show. Cards cut in the fragment shader for leaves and sorrel, small meshes textured from their world position for the rest. At the start about 2,900 of them, some 210,000 vertices.
- **Boulders** (`boulders.ts`, after the splash): if there are little stones in the moss there should be big ones. Day 05's boulder (`rock.ts`: a convex hull of points on a squashed sphere, subdivided, its edges rounded), grown over with the same moss as the ground (`dressSurface`): moss over the top and down the sides in patches, pincushion domes on the crown, a little reindeer lichen and haircap, the grey stone showing on the steep and the under sides, with crust lichen in irregular patches finely broken at their edge, in some places and not others, a slightly paler rim, now and then a yellow-green map lichen (one noise at one threshold had made round pale blots like dabs of paint, and in the low sun they went a bright mint). Fronds only in the thick of the moss and facing up (`dressSurface` takes a frond weight now): at its thin edge down a steep side they hung like a dark curtain of hair. Four templates built once (the hull, the subdivision and the baked cavity are the slow part); each boulder one of them turned, scaled, a metre and a half to three across (now and then one of four or more), set a fifth of its height into the ground, its moss fields worked out where it stands. On a 7 m grid out to 15 m (10 on a phone), more in the clearings, none by a trunk or at the start; fronds only on the ones within five and a half metres, added as you come close, left out of the shadow map. Solid, as the trunks.
- **Fungarium** (`fungarium.ts`): a little field notebook, opened by tapping the packet in your hand (a notebook icon bottom left did it for a while, then one by the packet; the packet itself is simpler). It comes up out of the packet and grows: the olive cover round a page of field-book paper (a CSS grid of pale blue lines over warm paper, shaded into the binding, the pages under it at the edge). One cell per species with a specimen rendered once into it, Swedish and Latin names, set as in day 05's moss (the Swedish in a small monospace, the Latin in an italic serif, long words allowed to break, since the Inter capitals ran out of the cell), the key that takes it; the cell itself takes it and shuts the book (picking was a second page away before, and you pick far more often than you look). With the book open, a number takes that species and shuts it, and the side arrows turn the pages: the grid, then a page for each species, as far as there are pages (the keys are in its header on a desktop). The magnifier in the corner opens the specimen on its own small renderer and scene, lit like the forest at dusk without the shadow map, turning slowly while it grows from nothing; "Grow again" replays it, "Throw these" makes it what tapping throws. The canvas is kept square and at most half the screen tall, so the names and buttons always show.
- **Slingshot** (`slingshot.ts`): sowing and flinging are both done with a slingshot, as a shooter's weapon. It is carried as a hand would hold it, out of the way: low, right of the middle (left of it on a phone, where the pouch is close), only the tips over the bottom edge, leaning and turned in a little, placed by the view's size so it sits the same on any screen (held in the middle it covered too much, on a phone above all). A quick click shoots from there; held down to draw, it straightens as it is drawn, as far as it is drawn (let go at half a draw and it was half way up), toward the middle under the sight but never all the way: three quarters up and still leaning a little at full draw; a little slower down again after the shot. A phone never draws, so there it stays low. A forked hazel twig of gnome size, whittled: each piece a tube swept along a curve with its radius carved point by point (so the cuts show in the outline and catch the light), coloured per vertex. The arms keep their bark, in grey-brown ridges with dark fissures along the grain and pale lenticels across, a knot where a twig was taken off, peeled near the tips with a notch cut for the bands; the crotch a swelling of smoother bark (the fissures crossing there were a jumble). The handle is peeled to honey wood: right under the fork a band of chip carving, three rows of six triangles round, pointing up and down in turn, cut deepest at their centres, the fresh faces pale and the bottoms dark (nine round in two rows were tall slivers that read as stripes), between two V grooves; a leather thong wound round the bottom for a grip; the cut ends pale end grain. Red rubber bands wound round under the tips to a cupped patch of leather, the pinch of spores peeking over its edge. Held, the pouch is drawn back at you (about five and a half centimetres, a little down), the bands stretching thinner and the load glowing; let go and it is a spring that snaps out past the fork and quivers back, the fork kicks, and a quarter of a second later it drops out of view, super quick, is filled while out of sight and comes back up. A click (a sowing) and a flick on a phone fire it as if drawn halfway. Swapping the spores drops it and brings it up again with the new ones. The pinch flung leaves from where the pouch is. It bobs and sways with the steps and lags behind the turn of the head. Drawn on its own after everything else, with its own camera at the eye (the depth of field and the haze leave it alone, it never goes into the moss), into a 4× multisampled target since the scene has no antialiasing, then laid over the screen; lit as in your own shadow: held just in front of you, low in the moss, the sun hardly ever falls on it, so mostly a dim warm fill, and the sun only from behind it when you look into the sun, lighting its edges (lit from the front by the sun it looked pasted on, bright against a wood in shade); the load in shade too until it is drawn. Hidden in the fungarium and with ?clean.
- **Pouch** (`pouch.ts`): an SVG kraft-paper packet with a folded top and a bit of twine, a field-book label on it with the fungarium's specimen, its Swedish and Latin names and its number. Only the one in hand shows. A swap is driven per frame: down out of view in 0.14 s easing in, the label changed while it is out of sight, up in 0.22 s popping up and settling; pressing again mid-swap just changes what comes up. It bobs in a small figure eight with the gait, and jerks up and tips on a throw. Digit keys by `code`, so shift still reads as the number; the wheel takes one notch per 140 ms. Kept in step with the fungarium both ways.
- **Gait**: two feet, a step each side (an ant's tripod patter for a while, before that a slow worm's heave): the step phase runs faster with speed, each push-off gives a small forward shove (the speed pulses ±35%); the eye rises over each step and dips as the foot comes down, about five millimetres, sways two and a half to the side the weight is on and rolls a little with it, and the head nods with each footfall. Walking, the head also looks about on its own, slowly, a few degrees this way and that and a little up and down, easing in and out as you start and stop. Standing, a slow breath lifts the eye and the head with it (quick little flicks of the head, as an ant's, were tried and dropped). A hop is thrown up at 1.3 m/s and falls back under gravity, and on landing the eye dips a centimetre on a spring and comes back. Only the eye moves, not where you are or aim; none of it with reduced motion asked for.
- **Fungi** (`fungi.ts`): clusters rebuilt while they grow (30 times a second, 12 on a phone: every frame, a lathe and its beads each time, a sown cluster stuttered a phone; the shadow map is drawn again only when one appears, finishes or goes, not for every step), 8 to 14 seconds each, started one after another over the first few seconds, with a speed that comes in little pulses. They grow in phases, as in a timelapse (see `process/ref/`): a pale knob pushes up out of the moss, the stem stretches with the cap still shut over it like a domed button, then the cap opens, its rim unrolling and the dome sinking into a dip (a chanterelle) or a funnel (a yellowfoot). A yellowfoot's slender stem shoots up before it opens, a chanterelle grows up with its cap. Pins come up nearly white and colour in as they grow. A puffball is a white knob under the moss that pushes up, swells into a ball, and last lifts on its foot. A growing finger's tip swells into a soft club that settles as it stops. Beads of water come out on fresh growth and stay (a finger's side below the tip, a funnel's rim, a chanterelle's top, a puffball's shoulder), drawn with the moss's dew: a child mesh of each cluster, built again with it. What you grow stays, up to 300 (100 on a phone); past that the oldest withers away, one for each new one (on a timer, a minute or two after growing, for a while). The ones further than 9 m are hidden and not built while they grow, caught up when they come back into view, so hundreds can stand about. Withering: over sixteen seconds the beads dry up, the colours go dark brown, caps roll in again, stems bend over from the top, fingers sag, puffballs go slack and flatten, and it all sinks into the moss. A module-level `ROT` set around each build carries it into the build functions. Fly agarics: one or two, sometimes three, standing apart, a lathe each: a stem on a bulb with ragged rings of veil, the cap's underside of white gills, and the cap a piece of a sphere that opens from a dome with its rim tucked under to nearly flat. It comes up as a white egg; the stem stretches and the red cap breaks through the veil, which stays on it as white warts, cells laid flat over the cap that start covering it all and shrink to spots, so they spread apart as the cap grows (how much veil is left rides in the part number, 5 to 5.9). The partial veil first spreads from the stem to the rim over the gills, then tears and hangs as a skirt. Finger fungi: 5 to 14 thin tubes out of one tight foot, each a path that leans out, curls over and wanders with two sines, tapering, swelling a little here and there, a rounded tip that is paler while it grows; about half fork into two arms. While growing, each tip circles slowly (nutation, as in a timelapse) and settles when done. White, golden or orange per cluster. Funnel chanterelles (yellowfoot): 2 to 7 per cluster, a lathe profile that is a slender hollow stem first, then flares into a funnel whose rim starts rolled in and opens out wavy (three sines around), the inside dark grey-brown going down into the hollow stem, the underside pale grey with forking false gills, the stem yellow-ochre and sinuous, swaying while it grows. Funnels in a cluster do not grow through each other, and there is no physics in it: each funnel's final shape is sliced into layers 3 mm apart (the axis, with its lean and sway, and the outer radius of stem, flare and waving rim), and each new one is tried against the ones already placed, layer by layer. If it would go into one by more than a light touch, it leans away from that one, and every other try goes taller or shorter, so the funnels sit at different heights, and gets a smaller funnel; after fourteen tries it is left out. Feet may fuse, as they do. Since it is the grown shape that is tested, they never cross while growing either. Chanterelles: the same lathe and layout, made solid: 1 to 3 per cluster, squat, a stem tapering down into the moss, a fleshy cap with a shallow dip and a thick rolled wavy rim, egg-yolk yellow all through with blunt forking ridges running down the stem. Puffballs: 2 to 6 per cluster set out so they do not touch, each a lathe that is a round, slightly flattened head on a foot narrowing into the moss (a smooth max of the two so there is no crease), with a little point on top; the head covered in warts, each a cell whose normal tilts off a small cone, the foot pleated. Older clusters are browner and open a dark pore at the top. (Tapping or crawling into one used to squash it and puff out spores; it went, fun but not needed.) Slime: the normal is perturbed by a slowly moving noise for a film of water that glistens, a sharp and a broad specular lobe, a grazing sheen, light through the thin flesh. Capped fungi (`buildCapped`): the fly agaric's lathe without its veil, in a style per species (the stem's swelling, the cap's thickness and how far it opens, what is under it). Karljohan (Boletus edulis): one to three, a fat club of a stem with a fine raised white net at the top (cells in the shader), a thick brown bun of a cap, paler at the rim, finely wrinkled, and under it a sponge of pores (tiny round mouths) instead of gills; it comes up all stem, a cork with a small cap pressed on it. Smörsopp (Suillus luteus): two to five under pine, a slimy chestnut cap with a low hump that opens flatter (three times the gloss), yellow pores, and a pale veil over them that tears into a violet-brown ring hanging on the stem, set below the rim or the rim hides it; the stem yellowish, dotted brown above the ring. Toppmurkla (Morchella elata, `buildMorel`): one to four, a tall blunt cone straight on a pale hollow stem, wider and furrowed at the foot, with a little groove where they meet; the cone's honeycomb is all shader, cells stretched upward and finer towards the tip, edges between side-by-side cells drawn as the long dark ridges and the pits olive brown and sunk in (the normal tilted into each). It comes up a small dark cone on almost no stem. Sotvaxskivling (Hygrophorus camarophyllus): two to five under spruce, the capped lathe with gills instead of pores (a style with `decurrent`: the stem ends lower and the gills flare from down on it out to the rim), thick and far apart in the shader with dark gaps and a waxy gloss; the cap sooty grey-brown, darker over its broad hump, streaked with fine fibres running out, greasy. Found clusters stand about on a 1.6 unit grid by hash. Sizes are real ones, a unit being a metre (the eye is 11 cm over the moss): crested coral 2 to 6 cm tall, yellowfoot 3 to 8, chanterelles 4 to 7 with caps 6 to 10 across, puffballs 4 to 7, and fly agarics the biggest thing on the floor, caps 10 to 17 cm across on stems as tall or taller. Measured from the grown geometry's bounds over forty seeds each; fingers had been twice too tall and fly agarics no taller than a yellowfoot.
- **Loading** (as day 12's ink drawing): the splash (`public/splash.webp`, a gnome with a slingshot among fly agarics in a sunlit birch wood) as a card in the middle, over the forest drawn as a faint golden wire mesh on near black: the scene rendered with one wireframe material over everything (so all in its plain geometry, the moss's shells one sheet) and a fog into the dark, so it has depth. Once the models are in and the picture's shaders compiled (`compileAsync`), and the card has been up two and a half seconds, the card goes and the picture fades in over the mesh's last frame, kept in a canvas over the page. A tap on the card lets you in at once; the controls wait for the picture. None under `?clean`.
- **Fungi as models** (`model/*.py` → `public/*.glb`, `models.ts`): the fungi moving one kind at a time from built-while-they-grow to models, each one instanced mesh per kind with the same six shape keys in the same order. The fly agaric (`model/agaric.py`) came first. Made parametrically in Blender, run headless: about 10,000 vertices, the same in every stage, so its growth is shape keys (glTF morph targets) over the grown, open one: an egg in the moss, a button with the ring still spread over the gills, a half-open dome with the ring torn from the rim, and withered (shorter, leaning a little, the outer half of the cap bent down about a hinge halfway out, so it keeps its size: curling it on the sphere had blown it up into a bell). Two more keys are the grown one's other caps, mixed in per fungus as it opens: a round dome to the end, or flattened with the rim turned up and waving so the gills show (a third or so each, the rest open; the withering bends an upturned rim down again). The red goes from a deep scarlet to a red going orange from one to the next, a little, and every cap gets more orange out to the rim, the very edge yellowish. The gills run out to the rim as a pale band; going to nothing there, they had left a ring of teeth. A bulb with ragged rings of the veil, a cap with thick flesh rolling under at the rim, 104 real gills in three lengths, a ring hanging as a pleated skirt with a ragged hem, and 120 warts as flat, lumpy flakes on seven uneven corners (as little pyramids they read as grey pearls), bigger and closer in the middle; they keep their size and the cap grows out from under them, so they spread apart. Dry and cream in the shader, no slime on them; the stem white with faint torn bands below the ring; withering browns it all, the red to rust. All fly agarics are one instanced mesh; each one sets four weights, its place, lean and size, and its veil (all white on the egg, cracking off into patches in the shader), withering and red, and only when one grows, withers, comes or goes. Forty growing at once: 0.05 ms a frame in `Fungi.update` against 7 ms rebuilding them ring by ring, the heap growing 15 MB in six seconds against 53. Each one is heavier to draw, though: 9,400 triangles against about 2,500. The fungarium's thumbnails are drawn again once the model is in. `?procagaric` keeps the old one, to compare. The porcini next (`model/porcini.py`, on the helpers the later ones share in `model/fungus.py`): a fat barrel of a stem swollen low, under a thick bun of a cap with a rounded lip, the brown skin going round it to a narrow pale edge, and the tube layer bulging down under it and sunk round the stem; no gills, the pores a sponge in the shader, white when young, going yellow, then olive, and a fine white net over the top of the stem. Its egg is a knob, its button the cork a young porcini is; about 6,300 vertices. `?procfungi` keeps them all procedural.
- **Spores** (`spores.ts`): far too fine to see one by one, so a pinch is smoke. Pale with green in it, more in some grains than others, tinted after the tone map (before it, a dense cloud went white whatever its colour); the pinch in the slingshot's pouch is the same pale green. One instanced quad for two kinds: puffs, soft blobs with slowly turning wisps inside that swell as they spread and thin, and motes, specks kept at about a pixel that only glitter where the light catches them. A throw leaves a faint arc of smoke hanging along its path (six puffs per frame spread over the step, so it is one continuous wisp), bursts where it lands in a small explosion of puffs and spores flung out and up, and what is left of the cloud settles into a pale band lying low over the moss: some forty wide, flat puffs in an elongated patch, fading in as the burst clears, then drifting, lifting a little and spreading as they fade out over fifteen to twenty-five seconds, so for a while you can still see where it went. The air curls everything with a field of sines and a faint breeze. The smoke is drawn after the atmosphere pass, into the finished image, with a lighter haze of its own (in the atmosphere it took the haze of whatever was behind it and vanished at a distance), as soft particles against the scene's depth so it goes behind ferns and trunks, and with its own alpha blend leaving the target's alpha alone, since that holds the depth for the lens. Forward scattering: pale from the side, gold looking into the sun.
- **Gyro**: as in day 12, a button on touch devices; iOS asks permission from the tap. The phone's angles are made a quaternion (as three's old DeviceOrientationControls did, the screen's rotation included), and the heading and tilt of the way its back camera points turn the head by their change since the last reading, so dragging and turning the phone add up. (Read straight off the angles, the compass heading swung to and fro with the phone held upright, gimbal lock, and the view with it.) Looking nearly straight up or down the heading is left alone.
- **Budget**: measured with GPU timer queries at 2160 × 1350. The first, still version took about 160 ms a frame: three's bloom pass alone was 100 ms of it and went; the haze inside the DOF gather ran 56 times per pixel and moved to its own pass; full-size GTAO (17 ms) went to half size (3 ms); 16 shells became 10 and the strand lean and lump noise moved from the fragment to the vertex shader; the pixel ratio is 1 by default (`?dpr=1.5` or `2` for stills). The crawl with mushrooms and props runs at about 21 ms headless at 1440 × 900. A fine chunk takes 6 ms to build, up to 45 ms at worst: that is the hitch you sometimes feel.

## Learned

- A cylinder built (around) × (along) has its normals inside. The moss grew into the trunk and its top went black and flat. Check the winding before tuning the shading.
- Sixteen vertex attributes is the cap, and an instanced mesh spends four on its matrix. Pack the per-vertex floats into a vec4.
- Sun from behind a log means the whole foreground sits in its shadow: the scene looked flat until the sun moved to the side.
- The canopy spots have to be smaller than the frame, or the whole view is one big spot and nothing reads as dappled.
- Measure before guessing: rAF intervals in headless Chrome are noise, `EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2` around a hand-rendered frame gives real GPU time per pass. The guilty one was the pass I added last and thought least about.
- A worm's eye inside the moss is too much: the fronds turn into giant flat shards. Just over the tips, the moss reads as moss and the grass as trees.
- Steering by where the pointer is on the screen sends you in circles and turns the view as you aim; steering by how far the finger has slid from where it went down, only while crawling, does not. Dragging the head and holding to crawl keep looking, aiming and moving apart.
- A mushroom thrown at your own feet grows up through your face: keep a minimum distance.
- Pincushion moss is shells only; in big patches on the ground they read as flat green facets. It is off on the ground.
