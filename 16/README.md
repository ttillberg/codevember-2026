# 16 / Spore

2026-11-16

Crawl over an endless moss carpet with a worm's eye, at dusk in autumn, and throw spores. Day 05's moss, taken out of the white studio and laid over a forest floor that never ends: dark glittering wood-moss and haircap over bare humus, the sun nine degrees over the horizon ahead of you so every frond tip glows. Small fungi push up through the moss, wet and glistening: yellowfoot, crested coral, chanterelles, puffballs and fly agarics, all of them at home in mossy Swedish spruce forest. Ferns arch over the moss in stands you crawl in under, turned gold and rust, glowing when the low sun comes through them; you start under one. Spruce trunks rise out of the moss into a golden mist that lies on the ground. The plants that stood here before (grass, fallen leaves and needles, spore stalks, branches, boulders) were taken out to be done again from scratch, ferns and trunks first.

Drag to turn your head. Hold still to crawl where you look, drag while holding to steer (left turns left, up looks up); on a phone, held low on the screen (the bottom fifth) you back up instead. A tap or a click sows a pinch of spores where you clicked, where that point's ray meets the moss (higher on the screen further off, to the side to the side) but 45 cm to 1.6 m away (closer, they grew up out of focus); with the pointer locked, at the sight: no throw, a soft cloud let go just over the moss that settles there, there at once (puffs near full size from the start; the burst's swell from small read as a second's wait). To fling one further, hold the mouse button and let go (the ring of the sight opens as it winds up), flick a finger up (quick and mostly straight; the look it dragged goes back; a finger's long press is the crawl), or press space: it flies at the sight, locked in the middle of the screen, and arcs over the moss leaving a faint wisp of smoke, bursts where it lands, and leaves a pale band of smoke lying over the moss that slowly fades; when the cloud settles, a cluster of whatever you picked in the fungarium pushes up there, slowly, one after another, the tips feeling their way round as they grow, and a minute or two later it withers back into the moss. Tap the packet (or F) for the fungarium, a little field notebook: it opens on a page with a grid, one cell per species, and a tap on one is what your spores grow from then on; the small magnifier in a cell's corner turns the page to watch it grow up close, turning slowly, grow it again, and throw it. Tap a puffball, or crawl into one, and it squashes and puffs out a cloud of spores. You hold a kraft-paper packet of spores at the bottom of the screen (larger on a desktop), its label the specimen it grows, like a shooter's weapon: 1 to 5 (up to 9 and 0 as species come), the wheel swaps it, dropping it out of view and bringing the next one up as in Quake; shift and a number plants that species' specimen at the sight. You have six legs: no lumbering, a quick even patter. Just looking, a drag turns the world round; while crawling a drag steers, so drag left to turn left, and held out to the side you keep turning that way, like a stick, so a hairpin needs no second swipe. Crawl straight into a trunk and you climb it, up to two metres: on the bark you crawl where you look (facing the trunk, forward is up; facing away, down; to the side, round it), and you can look straight down at the moss and the ferns from above; climb down to the moss and you are off. On a desktop the pill bottom right (or L) locks the pointer: the mouse itself is the look as in a shooter, a click sows, holding and letting go flings, esc lets go; with a mouse you crawl with the keys. On a desktop the keys are listed bottom left, in thin outlined caps, dim until hovered (they were a line in the title plate). With the pointer locked, A and D (and the side arrows) step sideways instead of turning, as in a shooter. W A S D or the arrows work too (shift runs, twice the pace), and on a phone the button top right turns your head with the phone. `?walk` crawls by itself.

## How

- **Chunks** (`world.ts`): the floor is built in 1 × 1 squares around you, three levels of detail by distance: fine grids with 13k fronds near you; coarser grids with a few fronds further out; coarse shells only at the edge of the haze. Chunks behind you go, the ones ahead are built one per frame, nearest and in front first. Every field (height, moss, species, pillows, clumps) is a function of the world position, so chunks meet without seams and one rebuilt later comes back the same. Normals come from the height field by central differences, the moss's smoother normal from a wider stencil, so nothing needs the neighbours.
- **Moss**: day 05's engine: a shell undercoat (10 noise shells here, 16 in day 05; per-species height, density, sheen and palette blended by five weights per vertex) and instanced fronds bent along beziers in the vertex shader, at day 05's 175 % size. Nothing is painted: coverage, age and species come from noise fields, and each chunk fills its frond age texture once.
- **Dew** (`dew.ts`): drops caught in the moss at the frond tips, sitting in the folds of fern pinnae, hanging under their tips. Each drop is a quad turned to the eye and shaded as a small sphere of water: a dark rim where light bends away, a dim refracted inside, a hard glint of the sun off the front, and looking towards the sun a gold spot of light gathered through it, stronger in some drops than others. Never smaller than about a pixel and a half; the ones that would be fade instead of flickering. Premultiplied blending, so the body darkens what is behind and the glints add on top; the lens turns the ones out of focus into sparkling discs. Wet everything else: a sharp sheen on the fronds, the fern pinnae and the mushroom caps.
- **One for phones**: a spore particle dying during a frame has a life just below zero, and `Math.pow(negative, 1.5)` is NaN. That NaN alpha went to the GPU for one frame per particle: desktop GPUs dropped those pixels, an iPhone drew each one as an opaque black disc, so every throw left a blinking stream of black balls. The life is clamped at zero now. (Reversed `smoothstep` edges, undefined in GLSL, were fixed along the way too.)
- **Mist**: on top of the haze, a ground mist: an exponential layer 0.35 units deep integrated along each view ray from the camera's height to the hit's, lit with the haze's colour, so it glows towards the sun and thins out above the moss. Right around you the air is clear: the mist starts 0.6 units out and eases in over the next metre or so.
- **Dusk**: the sun at nine degrees, ahead and to the right. The light carries a low-sun colour through the grade, the shade a cooling violet-blue sky fill, the back light is twice as strong and golden. Light shafts in the atmosphere pass: each pixel marches 24 steps towards the sun on screen through the depth, counting how much of the way is open sky or far haze, so mushrooms and the moss's own rise cut dark wedges out of the glow; they fade out as the sun goes behind you.
- **Near the eye**: fronds dissolve in a fine dither when they come within a few centimetres, instead of filling the frame as big flat blurred shapes.
- **Autumn**: the moss palettes went down to a deep dark green, the air to a golden brown, the sun warmer, the sky fill less blue, the canopy gaps yellow and orange, and the grade warms the highlights.
- **Light**: a 4096 shadow map over the 6 units ahead of you, re-rendered every 0.3 units you crawl, snapped to whole texels so the shadows do not crawl with you, and without the fronds and stalks, too small to matter in it. A canopy field projected along the sun and swaying slowly breaks the light into pools and spots; shade drops to 22 %. The back light is the sun itself and only shows looking into it.
- **Lens**: GTAO at half resolution, then two full-screen passes on the beauty target's depth. The first rebuilds the world ray per pixel for a height and sun-aware haze and a canopy sky, and writes the view depth into alpha. The second gathers a 40-tap golden-angle depth of field (thin lens, the background never bleeding over sharp foreground), then grades: cool shadows, warm highlights, a vignette. The focus is where the view ray first hits the moss or a mushroom, eased like a lens hunting.
- **Ants** (`ants.ts`): carpenter ants (hästmyra, the biggest in Sweden, about 14 mm, black with a dark red thorax, nesting in spruce trunks) on trails from half the trunks near you, by hash: out over the moss's top 1.4 to 3 m, wandering either side of a straight line, and up the bark 1.8 m on the side facing it, drifting round a little. A trail is sampled every centimetre with its surface normal (the ground's slope, or out of the bark, using the trunk's own radius function). Fourteen to twenty-four ants a metre, each with its own lane a few millimetres off the line and its own pace, turning back at the ends, now and then stopping a moment. One instanced mesh: ellipsoids for gaster, petiole, thorax, head and mandibles, three-sided tubes for six legs and two elbowed antennae; the legs step in two tripods half a stride apart, swinging about their roots and lifting, the antennae feel about, all in the vertex shader from a per-instance stride phase. Glossy chitin, the moss's light and shadow. Not drawn past 4.5 m, nor into the shadow map. They are small at a worm's height: on the bark they show as a file of dark bodies; on the moss they are half hidden among the leaves, as they would be.
- **Sun at your back** (a cheat): with a sun nine degrees up, its long shadows cover most of the floor and front light barely reaches the moss's very dark top, so looking away from the sun was a black carpet while looking into it everything glowed. Now whatever is turned to the sun catches a warm gold of its own (not its dark colour lit), scaled by how much the sun is at your back: the strands' tips, the velvet under them, the fronds' leaves, fern pinnae and the fungi. In the shadows a few flecks still get through the fern lace (a finer noise projected along the light). The canopy lets a little more sun through, the deepest shade is 20% instead of 14%, and the fungi's fill and wet sheen follow the shade like the rest.
- **Grade**: one look over everything, at the end of the lens pass: greens pulled towards olive and calmed (the moss was a loud lime next to the golden ferns), unsaturated midtones, the haze, leaned amber instead of a flat green-grey, shadows toned a deep blue-teal and highlights a cream gold, a soft S on the luminance with a rounded shoulder so sunlit fronds do not burn out. The packet in your hand is graded with it in CSS: dimmer, warmer, a little sepia, its label off-white, a warm glow from the sun at its top left and shade at the bottom.
- **Climbing**: crawling into a trunk head on (pushed off it, facing it within about 40°, not looking down) puts you on it: an angle round it and a height over its foot. The eye sits a worm's height off the bark, from the same radius function the trunk's geometry is built with (root flare and buttresses included), and eases there, so getting on is a move. Forward is split by where you look: into the bark is up, away is down, sideways is round, plus the look's pitch; 0.7 times the pace on the ground. You can look down to about 83° while on it. Climbing down past the moss puts you back on the ground; the not-looking-down rule keeps it from catching you straight away again.
- **Crawling**: a worm's eye just over the frond tips, following the ground, with a slow heave and roll while moving. Mushroom stems are solid: you slide round them. A press becomes a crawl after 220 ms if it has not moved; past 8 px it is a head turn; a short press that did neither is a tap. With a mouse, dragging up looks up; with a finger, it drags the world (up looks down). A screen height of drag turns about twice the field of view (it was 1.2, too slow for a U-turn); crawling, the finger's distance sideways from where it went down, past 14 px, also turns you at up to 2.4 rad/s, full at 30% of the short side. The keys turn at 2.2 rad/s. With the pointer locked, mouse movement turns the head directly (1.6 fields of view per screen height), and the press is only ever a tap or a crawl.
- **Ferns** (`ferns.ts`): a crown of 6 to 11 fronds, each a quadratic bezier up out of the crown, arching over and sinking at the tip, the outer ones lower. A thin tapering rachis, then 16 pinnae down each side, alternating, longest a third of the way up; each pinna a strip three columns wide, folded up along its midrib and curving down under its own weight, about five times as long as wide. The pinnule outline is cut in the fragment shader from (along, across): lobes leaning towards the tip, cut almost to the midrib, a few teeth on each, so the edge is crisp without a vertex per lobe. Autumn per frond, the tips ahead of the base: green, gold, rust, brown. Under each pinnule, rows of rust spore dots (sori). Light from behind comes through. The air moves the fronds, more at the tip. Placed by hash in 2.6 unit cells, two to five to a stand, fronds up to two units long arching high enough to crawl under, merged into one mesh per cell, in the shadow map; only the tight bunch of stipes at a crown is solid. Two levels of detail: cells further than 4.5 units get half the pinnae, each nearly twice as wide, and fewer rows, rebuilt one cell per frame as you come and go.
- **Trunks** (`trees.ts`): one spruce in about half the cells of a 3.6 unit grid, by hash, out to 34 units. A unit trunk with its root flare (buttresses from two sines, sinking into the ground, rings packed towards the foot) in three levels of detail, 48 × 40, 16 × 14 and 8 × 3, each an instanced mesh with the same material; trunks are sorted into them every half step you take. The bark is all shader, spruce, after a photo of one: thin round scales two or three centimetres across overlapping like roof tiles, a cell noise with a whole number of cells around so it wraps without a seam, its cells warped by a noise sampled on a circle (so it wraps too) so the scales come out curved and uneven. Each scale rises to its lower edge, which stands off the trunk: where it lies over the scale below a dark gap shows, beside its neighbours only a faint parting, so they read as tiles and not a net of outlines (outlined all round they looked like crazy paving). One in thirty has come away, red-brown underneath. Dark grey, a shade different each, weathered unevenly up close with pale flaky patches and dark pits. The normal comes from finite differences of that height along the bark, and fades out with the relief and contrast as the scales shrink under a pixel, where the extra lookups are skipped. (It was pine, long plates and deep furrows, before that a uniform cell pattern that read as a tortoise shell; the plates looked fake up close.) Green algae on the shaded side, pale crust lichen. Up close, once a few millimetres are bigger than a pixel, the scales get a grain of their own: short fibres, small papery flakes and crumbs, bump-mapped and darkening between the fibres. Each trunk stands on a little hill of the ground's own moss: the ground height has a rounded rise at every trunk, a bit over ten centimetres at the foot of a big one and gone 90 cm past the bark, so the moss carpet heaps up and climbs the foot a little. The trunks are looked up by hash in the ground's own module so it knows where they stand, cached per cell. (A cushion of moss painted onto the bark, swelling its outline, looked like a green rug; the real moss is better.) Matte, not plastic: the lichen comes in specks, the sun does not reach into the gaps, and the rim of light from the sun behind is weak and broken up. Solid, root flare included; the autofocus takes the flare at its average, since with the full buttress it focused in the air in front of a trunk you stood next to.
- **Fungarium** (`fungarium.ts`): a little field notebook, opened by tapping the packet in your hand (a notebook icon bottom left did it for a while, then one by the packet; the packet itself is simpler). It comes up out of the packet and grows: the olive cover round a page of field-book paper (a CSS grid of pale blue lines over warm paper, shaded into the binding, the pages under it at the edge). One cell per species with a specimen rendered once into it, Swedish and Latin names, set as in day 05's moss (the Swedish in a small monospace, the Latin in an italic serif, long words allowed to break, since the Inter capitals ran out of the cell), the key that takes it; the cell itself takes it and shuts the book (picking was a second page away before, and you pick far more often than you look). With the book open, a number takes that species and shuts it, and the side arrows turn the pages: the grid, then a page for each species, as far as there are pages (the keys are in its header on a desktop). The magnifier in the corner opens the specimen on its own small renderer and scene, lit like the forest at dusk without the shadow map, turning slowly while it grows from nothing; "Grow again" replays it, "Throw these" makes it what tapping throws. The canvas is kept square and at most half the screen tall, so the names and buttons always show.
- **Pouch** (`pouch.ts`): an SVG kraft-paper packet with a folded top and a bit of twine, a field-book label on it with the fungarium's specimen, its Swedish and Latin names and its number. Only the one in hand shows. A swap is driven per frame: down out of view in 0.14 s easing in, the label changed while it is out of sight, up in 0.22 s popping up and settling; pressing again mid-swap just changes what comes up. It bobs in a small figure eight with the gait, and jerks up and tips on a throw. Digit keys by `code`, so shift still reads as the number; the wheel takes one notch per 140 ms. Kept in step with the fungarium both ways.
- **Gait**: a tripod gait, three legs down at a time: the step phase runs faster with speed (about five steps a second at full), each push-off gives a small forward shove (the speed pulses ±35%) and a tiny tick up, and the body rocks a few thousandths of a radian from one tripod to the other. Before it was a slow worm-like heave.
- **Fungi** (`fungi.ts`): clusters rebuilt while they grow (30 times a second, 12 on a phone: every frame, a lathe and its beads each time, a sown cluster stuttered a phone; the shadow map is drawn again only when one appears, finishes or goes, not for every step), 8 to 14 seconds each, started one after another over the first few seconds, with a speed that comes in little pulses. They grow in phases, as in a timelapse (see `process/ref/`): a pale knob pushes up out of the moss, the stem stretches with the cap still shut over it like a domed button, then the cap opens, its rim unrolling and the dome sinking into a dip (a chanterelle) or a funnel (a yellowfoot). A yellowfoot's slender stem shoots up before it opens, a chanterelle grows up with its cap. Pins come up nearly white and colour in as they grow. A puffball is a white knob under the moss that pushes up, swells into a ball, and last lifts on its foot. A growing finger's tip swells into a soft club that settles as it stops. Beads of water come out on fresh growth and stay (a finger's side below the tip, a funnel's rim, a chanterelle's top, a puffball's shoulder), drawn with the moss's dew: a child mesh of each cluster, built again with it. Thrown ones wither away a minute or two after they have grown (the ones found in the forest stay): over sixteen seconds the beads dry up, the colours go dark brown, caps roll in again, stems bend over from the top, fingers sag, puffballs go slack and flatten, and it all sinks into the moss. A module-level `ROT` set around each build carries it into the build functions. Fly agarics: one or two, sometimes three, standing apart, a lathe each: a stem on a bulb with ragged rings of veil, the cap's underside of white gills, and the cap a piece of a sphere that opens from a dome with its rim tucked under to nearly flat. It comes up as a white egg; the stem stretches and the red cap breaks through the veil, which stays on it as white warts, cells laid flat over the cap that start covering it all and shrink to spots, so they spread apart as the cap grows (how much veil is left rides in the part number, 5 to 5.9). The partial veil first spreads from the stem to the rim over the gills, then tears and hangs as a skirt. Finger fungi: 5 to 14 thin tubes out of one tight foot, each a path that leans out, curls over and wanders with two sines, tapering, swelling a little here and there, a rounded tip that is paler while it grows; about half fork into two arms. While growing, each tip circles slowly (nutation, as in a timelapse) and settles when done. White, golden or orange per cluster. Funnel chanterelles (yellowfoot): 2 to 7 per cluster, a lathe profile that is a slender hollow stem first, then flares into a funnel whose rim starts rolled in and opens out wavy (three sines around), the inside dark grey-brown going down into the hollow stem, the underside pale grey with forking false gills, the stem yellow-ochre and sinuous, swaying while it grows. Funnels in a cluster do not grow through each other, and there is no physics in it: each funnel's final shape is sliced into layers 3 mm apart (the axis, with its lean and sway, and the outer radius of stem, flare and waving rim), and each new one is tried against the ones already placed, layer by layer. If it would go into one by more than a light touch, it leans away from that one, and every other try goes taller or shorter, so the funnels sit at different heights, and gets a smaller funnel; after fourteen tries it is left out. Feet may fuse, as they do. Since it is the grown shape that is tested, they never cross while growing either. Chanterelles: the same lathe and layout, made solid: 1 to 3 per cluster, squat, a stem tapering down into the moss, a fleshy cap with a shallow dip and a thick rolled wavy rim, egg-yolk yellow all through with blunt forking ridges running down the stem. Puffballs: 2 to 6 per cluster set out so they do not touch, each a lathe that is a round, slightly flattened head on a foot narrowing into the moss (a smooth max of the two so there is no crease), with a little point on top; the head covered in warts, each a cell whose normal tilts off a small cone, the foot pleated. Older clusters are browner and open a dark pore at the top. Pressed (a tap, or crawling into it) it squashes and springs back, and a puff of spores comes out of the pore. Slime: the normal is perturbed by a slowly moving noise for a film of water that glistens, a sharp and a broad specular lobe, a grazing sheen, light through the thin flesh. Found clusters stand about on a 1.6 unit grid by hash. Sizes are real ones, a unit being a metre (the eye is 11 cm over the moss): crested coral 2 to 6 cm tall, yellowfoot 3 to 8, chanterelles 4 to 7 with caps 6 to 10 across, puffballs 4 to 7, and fly agarics the biggest thing on the floor, caps 10 to 17 cm across on stems as tall or taller. Measured from the grown geometry's bounds over forty seeds each; fingers had been twice too tall and fly agarics no taller than a yellowfoot.
- **Spores** (`spores.ts`): far too fine to see one by one, so a pinch is smoke. One instanced quad for two kinds: puffs, soft blobs with slowly turning wisps inside that swell as they spread and thin, and motes, specks kept at about a pixel that only glitter where the light catches them. A throw leaves a faint arc of smoke hanging along its path (six puffs per frame spread over the step, so it is one continuous wisp), bursts where it lands in a small explosion of puffs and spores flung out and up, and what is left of the cloud settles into a pale band lying low over the moss: some forty wide, flat puffs in an elongated patch, fading in as the burst clears, then drifting, lifting a little and spreading as they fade out over fifteen to twenty-five seconds, so for a while you can still see where it went. The air curls everything with a field of sines and a faint breeze. The smoke is drawn after the atmosphere pass, into the finished image, with a lighter haze of its own (in the atmosphere it took the haze of whatever was behind it and vanished at a distance), as soft particles against the scene's depth so it goes behind ferns and trunks, and with its own alpha blend leaving the target's alpha alone, since that holds the depth for the lens. Forward scattering: pale from the side, gold looking into the sun.
- **Gyro**: as in day 12, a button on touch devices; iOS asks permission from the tap. The phone's angles are made a quaternion (as three's old DeviceOrientationControls did, the screen's rotation included), and the heading and tilt of the way its back camera points turn the head by their change since the last reading, so dragging and turning the phone add up. (Read straight off the angles, the compass heading swung to and fro with the phone held upright, gimbal lock, and the view with it.) Looking nearly straight up or down the heading is left alone.
- **Budget**: measured with GPU timer queries at 2160 × 1350. The first, still version took about 160 ms a frame: three's bloom pass alone was 100 ms of it and went; the haze inside the DOF gather ran 56 times per pixel and moved to its own pass; full-size GTAO (17 ms) went to half size (3 ms); 16 shells became 10 and the strand lean and lump noise moved from the fragment to the vertex shader; the pixel ratio is 1 by default (`?dpr=1.5` or `2` for stills). The crawl with mushrooms and props runs at about 21 ms headless at 1440 × 900. A fine chunk takes 6 ms to build, up to 45 ms at worst: that is the hitch you sometimes feel.

## Learned

- A cylinder built (around) × (along) has its normals inside. The moss grew into the trunk and its top went black and flat. Check the winding before tuning the shading.
- Sixteen vertex attributes is the cap, and an instanced mesh spends four on its matrix. Pack the per-vertex floats into a vec4.
- Sun from behind a log means the whole foreground sits in its shadow: the scene looked flat until the sun moved to the side.
- The canopy spots have to be smaller than the frame, or the whole view is one big spot and nothing reads as dappled.
- Measure before guessing: rAF intervals in headless Chrome are noise, `EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2` around a hand-rendered frame gives real GPU time per pass. The guilty one was the pass I added last and thought least about.
- A worm's eye inside the moss is too much: the fronds turn into giant flat shards. Just over the tips, the moss reads as moss and the grass as trees.
- Steering by where the pointer is on the screen sends you in circles and turns the view as you aim; steering by how far the finger has slid from where it went down, only while crawling, does not. Dragging the head and holding to crawl keep looking, aiming and moving apart.
- A mushroom thrown at your own feet grows up through your face: keep a minimum distance.
- Pincushion moss is shells only; in big patches on the ground they read as flat green facets. It is off on the ground.
