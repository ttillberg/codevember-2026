# 08 / Tesseract

2026-11-08

TESSERAKT, a hypercube built for real. A tesseract cannot exist in three dimensions, but its shadow can: turned in 4D and seen in perspective, it is a 3D object whose edges change length and nothing else. So that is the machine. 32 telescopic struts in three stages, sleeve, stanchion, rod, on 16 ball joints, with one crank turning it a quarter at a time through the XW, YW or ZW plane: a glide of a quarter at each catch, or with AUTOTRANSFORM a swing to and fro (a hard mode, an even turn broken now and then by a servo in quick steps, snap, overshoot, settle, was dropped). Radii are constant like on a real part, only the lengths move. Painted sand and graphite with decals, silver rods, black seals, a coloured ring here and there, six blinking LEDs, a quad drone living in the cells with its own camera in the corner, on a soft gradient. Drag orbits the camera round it; the crank turns round on its own every 8 to 22 s. `?clean` hides the page furniture.

## How

- **One plane at a time** (`state.ts`): the machine turns in XW, YW or ZW, never two at once. Compose turns in two planes and an edge can point straight at the eye; its strut would have to shrink to nothing. But after a quarter turn the tesseract stands on itself again, a cell has moved to the next place, so the next quarter can go in any plane. The pose is a base of whole quarter turns, a signed permutation of the axes, and one live angle in one plane on top. In one plane the shortest strut is 0.73 and the longest 3.03 (`node` sweep over the whole turn), and no two joints that are not neighbours come closer than 1.0. Seeing it from other sides is a 3D job: orbit.
- **Servo** (`state.ts`): a spring on the crank angle (ω ≈ 12.6, ζ 0.68), so a step lands with one small overshoot and no wobble. Now it only settles a turn by hand on its quarter; in the dropped hard mode, every 6 to 14 s the even spin stops dead and the servo takes over for a burst, a snap to the next quarter the way it was turning, then one to three more quarter turns, the tesseract's own period, half of them whole and the rest staggered in two or three uneven lurches a breath apart. Landed and settled, the quarters are banked, the plane changes and the spin picks up again from still.
- **Struts** (`Machine.tsx`): per edge, a sleeve sunk into each ball, a stanchion sliding out of each sleeve as far as the strut needs up to 0.8, and a rod between the stanchions taking the rest. Each stage overlaps the one round it by 0.1 so nothing shows daylight. Two sleeves fully compressed are 0.65 with the balls, under the shortest strut. Seals are short fat rings at every mouth.
- **Colour**: the eight edges along w are sand (white was tried for a while), the other 24 graphite, so you can follow which edges are which as the inner cube turns out and the outer turns in.
- **Decals** (`decals.ts`): canvas textures on the cylinder UVs, not `DecalGeometry`. One sleeve in three has one, the rest are plain paint; x runs round the tube the way a viewer outside reads it, y along it, the open end at the top; the far sleeve of a strut hangs the other way up, so its lettering is turned round. Each carries a few of: hazard band at the mouth, brand round the tube (CDVMBR 08, three.js with its triangle mark, pmndrs), serial along it, barcode, a coloured tab, the three.js mark as pictogram. Seeded, so the same every load.
- **Assembly marks** (`Machine.tsx`): a parting line round every ball with two screws on it, a groove round every sleeve by the ball with a screw beside it. A screw is a steel disc flush with the surface and a darker hex socket in it; the eye reads the recess. All instanced, four draw calls for 272 marks.
- **Device** (`Drones.tsx`, `device.ts`, modelled in Blender, `model/device.blend`, exported to `public/device.glb`): a ball with one principal eye, a graphite core, a blue collar, two green ring pods on its sides, and round it four shell petals hinged at the back: its walls, its wings. In flight they are half shut, spread wide only for the catch, from closing in on the piece till it is taken; going through a face they swing forward and shut the ball with only the eye out, and open again on the far side. Eight blades round the eye are its shutter, a blink at every crossing and now and then on its own. The hinges are nodes in the file, each turned by one angle from code. It lives in the tesseract on its boundary: inside one of the eight cells, a cube with one coordinate held at ±1 and three free, and can only leave through a square face into the neighbouring cell. It goes for a piece: a small hexagonal bit of steel, set in a cell two walls away, never the one it is in; the plan to it is two faces, each placed a little towards the piece on the axes the cells share, then the last stretch, each step made into a leg from wherever the device is when its turn comes, so the flight is one continuous curve. The catch runs in phases: the device holds still in front of the piece for a moment, eye on it, the scoop out; the grip is a scoop as for ice cream, a steel bowl on an arm out of the eye cap with a blade nested in it, which swings over the bowl's mouth on two pins and shuts the piece in, and the scoop goes home shut, the piece swallowed, while the shutter blinks; then the machine glides one quarter turn, eased in and out over two seconds, its only movement, while the next piece emerges two walls off; a half second still, and off again. Between catches the tesseract holds its shape. The next one emerges two walls off, and in the cam the piece sits in its own room of the net, by its cell's map. Through a face a leg is one smooth curve, a two-piece cubic Bezier in 4D with the face at its middle. It flies square into the face and square on out of it, so the turn is the 4D right angle itself, taken in the face; every control point stays on the boundary inside its cell, so the path never bulges out of the tesseract. It flies at a constant speed, by distance along the path (each leg keeps a table of length against its Bezier parameter, which runs fast on the straight and slow in the bends), through walls and all, straight on (for a moment it eased into the face, held a quarter second there with no smoke and no laser, and eased out, so a crossing read as in and out rather than a bounce; dropped, direct it is); the laser shows nothing of its first second behind the device and fades in over the next 1.2, so it starts a way back in the wake, not at the tail; only the last stretch to the piece slows, over its last 0.4, to a quarter, for the catch. A dip of a third at the face, then a rush through it at two and five times, were tried. The legs are rounder sweeps: the heading carries 0.45 of a leg into it, and a cruise has no random knot in its middle. A Gaussian field round the face still shuts the wings and the ball. At the middle the held coordinate swaps with the face's, and the face itself, the square between its four joints, carries a honeycomb of hairlines in the colour of the cell beyond as the device heads for it (cells 0.012 across a wall 2 across, ten times finer than at first, some 33,000 edges worked out once on the wall and cut to its square; too many to put through the turn on the CPU, so each end keeps its place on the wall and the vertex shader puts it through the turn and the w perspective, from the wall's middle and its two ways along it, turned, as uniforms; grid and ripple share the one geometry, and the frame time does not move. Up close a fine mesh, from afar it reads as a tinted pane), and after the crossing the ripple runs through that honeycomb, each edge lighting as the wave from the crossing reaches it, a faint wash behind (two hairline rings before, a filled ring in a shader before them); in the eye's view the old filled flash, for now. Never straight back out the way it came. Position and trail are points in 4D seen through the same turn and perspective as the struts: what reads as speed is partly the warp of the cell it is in, and the trail bends as the machine turns. Its orientation lives in cell space, the way it is going with an up carried from frame to frame, or the pilot's; a cell's map takes the eye's forward and up to 4D and the projection at the device gives the 3D pose. Through a wall it is two copies: in 4D the half of the body past the wall lies in the neighbour's space and projects with the neighbour's turn, so a second copy is posed by the neighbour's map and the two are clipped against the wall's plane, this side and the far side, and fold at it. Before it, a quad drone with rotors. Its size in the picture, and the piece's with it, is two thirds of what it was at first, made a third and then twice that (SIZE_MAIN, PIECE_R): the reach, the catch and the hand-over at a wall go by its radius and come down with it; the eye's view keeps its own size.

## Learned

- Perspective along w is what gives the w-edges length at all; in parallel projection they vanish. The eye distance is a trade between long w-edges (near) and a tame length range (far).
- `setFromUnitVectors(Y, dir)` and the same quaternion times `(1,0,0,0)` gives the strut's two ends the same roll, so decals on both sleeves line up. The far end is upside down though, and upside-down lettering looks mirrored at a glance: check the UVs before flipping the canvas.
