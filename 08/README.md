# 08 / Tesseract

2026-11-08

TESSERAKT, a hypercube built for real. A tesseract cannot exist in three dimensions, but its shadow can: turned in 4D and seen in perspective, it is a 3D object whose edges change length and nothing else. So that is the machine. 32 telescopic struts in three stages, sleeve, stanchion, rod, on 16 ball joints, with one crank turning it a quarter at a time through the XW, YW or ZW plane, evenly, or, on M, a servo doing it in steps the way a first prototype moves: hold, snap, overshoot, settle. Radii are constant like on a real part, only the lengths move. Painted sand and graphite with decals, silver rods, black seals, a coloured ring here and there, six blinking LEDs, a quad drone living in the cells with its own camera in the corner, on a soft gradient. Space stops the crank, the arrows turn it by hand, drag turns the machine itself, the camera stands still and straight on, and sets which way it cranks, and it turns round on its own every 8 to 22 s, M swaps the even spin for a stepping servo. `?clean` hides the page furniture.

## How

- **One plane at a time** (`state.ts`): the machine turns in XW, YW or ZW, never two at once. Compose turns in two planes and an edge can point straight at the eye; its strut would have to shrink to nothing. But after a quarter turn the tesseract stands on itself again, a cell has moved to the next place, so the next quarter can go in any plane. The pose is a base of whole quarter turns, a signed permutation of the axes, and one live angle in one plane on top. In one plane the shortest strut is 0.73 and the longest 3.03 (`node` sweep over the whole turn), and no two joints that are not neighbours come closer than 1.0. Seeing it from other sides is a 3D job: orbit.
- **Servo** (`state.ts`): a stiff spring on the crank angle (ω ≈ 19, ζ 0.68), so a step lands with one small overshoot and no wobble. The program steps a quarter turn at a time, the tesseract's own period: every hold is the same shape with the colours moved on. Half the steps come whole, the rest stagger in two or three uneven lurches with a tenth of a second between, then a hold of 0.9 to 2.7 s. One in ten goes back. The arrows move the target, the spring does the rest.
- **Struts** (`Machine.tsx`): per edge, a sleeve sunk into each ball, a stanchion sliding out of each sleeve as far as the strut needs up to 0.8, and a rod between the stanchions taking the rest. Each stage overlaps the one round it by 0.1 so nothing shows daylight. Two sleeves fully compressed are 0.65 with the balls, under the shortest strut. Seals are short fat rings at every mouth.
- **Colour**: the eight edges along w are sand, the other 24 graphite, so you can follow which edges are which as the inner cube turns out and the outer turns in.
- **Decals** (`decals.ts`): canvas textures on the cylinder UVs, not `DecalGeometry`. One sleeve in three has one, the rest are plain paint; x runs round the tube the way a viewer outside reads it, y along it, the open end at the top; the far sleeve of a strut hangs the other way up, so its lettering is turned round. Each carries a few of: hazard band at the mouth, brand round the tube (CDVMBR 08, three.js with its triangle mark, pmndrs), serial along it, barcode, a coloured tab, the three.js mark as pictogram. Seeded, so the same every load.
- **Assembly marks** (`Machine.tsx`): a parting line round every ball with two screws on it, a groove round every sleeve by the ball with a screw beside it. A screw is a steel disc flush with the surface and a darker hex socket in it; the eye reads the recess. All instanced, four draw calls for 272 marks.
- **Device** (`Drones.tsx`, `device.ts`, modelled in Blender, `model/device.blend`, exported to `public/device.glb`): a ball with one principal eye, a graphite core, a blue collar, two green ring pods on its sides, and round it four shell petals hinged at the back: its walls. Open they trail like fins; going through a face they swing forward and shut the ball with only the eye out, and open again on the far side. Eight blades round the eye are its shutter, a blink at every crossing and now and then on its own. The hinges are nodes in the file, each turned by one angle from code. It lives in the tesseract on its boundary: inside one of the eight cells, a cube with one coordinate held at ±1 and three free, and can only leave through a square face into the neighbouring cell. It goes for a piece: a small hexagonal bit of steel, set in a cell two walls away, never the one it is in; the plan to it is two faces, each placed a little towards the piece on the axes the cells share, then the last stretch, each step made into a leg from wherever the device is when its turn comes, so the flight is one continuous curve. The catch runs in phases: the device holds still in front of the piece for a moment, eye on it, the grip out; the grip takes it, the piece shrinks into the ring and the shutter blinks; then the machine glides one quarter turn, eased in and out over two seconds, its only movement, while the next piece emerges two walls off; a half second still, and off again. Between catches the tesseract holds its shape (space runs the old program again, m makes its turn even). The next one emerges two walls off, and in the cam the piece sits in its own room of the net, by its cell's map. Through a face a leg is one smooth curve, a two-piece cubic Bezier in 4D with the face at its middle. It flies square into the face and square on out of it, so the turn is the 4D right angle itself, taken in the face; every control point stays on the boundary inside its cell, so the path never bulges out of the tesseract. A Gaussian field round the face eases the speed down by a third and gives it back. At the middle the held coordinate swaps with the face's, and the face itself, the square between its four joints, flashes green just before, in both views. Never straight back out the way it came. Position and trail are points in 4D seen through the same turn and perspective as the struts: what reads as speed is partly the warp of the cell it is in, and the trail bends as the machine turns. Its orientation lives in cell space, the way it is going with an up carried from frame to frame, or the pilot's; a cell's map takes the eye's forward and up to 4D and the projection at the device gives the 3D pose. Through a wall it is two copies: in 4D the half of the body past the wall lies in the neighbour's space and projects with the neighbour's turn, so a second copy is posed by the neighbour's map and the two are clipped against the wall's plane, this side and the far side, and fold at it. Before it, a quad drone with rotors.
- **At the controls** (`p`): take the device over. It keeps the way it was going and from then on has momentum in cell space: the pointer over the stage is the look, as in a first-person game, a held button or `space` the thrust along the eye against drag; `w` `s` `a` `d` and the arrows still work, eased rates, nothing snaps. The eye cam looks where the eye points. Through a wall it is the same crossing as on its own, the room beyond is the next cell, and the wall ahead lights as it closes in; the field round the walls slows it and shuts the ball as it does on its own. `p` again lets go and it carries on from there by itself. The crossing rule in `drone.ts` moves the net by the wall's side in cell space, not its 4D sign: a row can run its axis backwards after a crossing, and the old rule put the next room on the wrong side then.
- **Eye cam** (`DroneCam.tsx`, `drone.ts`): a second scene in a scissored viewport, bottom right, a square unlabelled frame that is always there, `?clean` too, from the device's eye. It renders cell space: the cube the device is in, rigid struts, no telescopes, and a box of 5 × 5 × 5 rooms round it continuing straight on through the faces as in a net, instanced, so when the net recentres on a new room the only cubes born are a whole slab on the far side, four units out at the least, already in the fog. From inside, a tesseract is just rooms. The eye is a 180 degree fisheye: the room is rendered into a cube map from the eye's position each frame and unwrapped onto the frame by an equidistant lens, a right angle out to each side edge, so the view is a disc in the square, fading to paper past it; fog and tone mapping as in the main picture. The view keeps its own up vector from frame to frame and only leans back to the room's up while looking level, so a leg straight up or down a room never flips it; the orientation is slerped so no frame can snap. Cell space is an affine map from 4D, one row per free axis; on crossing a face the row that mapped the face's axis is remade to map the axis that was held, pointing inward and shifted two cells, so the view carries on without a jump. Edges along w are sand here too, and exponential fog the colour of the backdrop lets the rooms beyond fade. The main scene's environment is shared. Hidden in `?clean`.
- **Lasers**, tried and dropped: beams fixed in the room through the machine, cast against the 24 projected faces and jogged along each wall's projected 4D normal, three layers for a fake bloom, shown in the drone cam too. Handsome in stills, but it fought the machine for the eye. Lives in the history before `08/src/Laser.tsx` was removed.
- **LEDs** (`Machine.tsx`): six status lights on the balls, each with its own blink: heartbeat, breathe, a flash every five seconds, fast steady, slow square, tick. A pinhead emissive bead in a black collar, a few pixels, no glow.
- **Light**: drei `Environment` from four `Lightformer`s, no HDR download, plus one directional. `ContactShadows` on a transparent canvas; behind it a soft radial gradient in CSS, light at the machine, darker at the edges. Tone mapping is `NeutralToneMapping` at exposure 1: ACES darkened the sand and greyed the accents, Neutral keeps the paint near its hex values, which is the point of a flat-paint look.

## Learned

- Perspective along w is what gives the w-edges length at all; in parallel projection they vanish. The eye distance is a trade between long w-edges (near) and a tame length range (far).
- `setFromUnitVectors(Y, dir)` and the same quaternion times `(1,0,0,0)` gives the strut's two ends the same roll, so decals on both sleeves line up. The far end is upside down though, and upside-down lettering looks mirrored at a glance: check the UVs before flipping the canvas.
